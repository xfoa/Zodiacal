# Zodiacal Worldbuilding Notes

A working record of the world as decided: what it looks like, how it works, where the conflicts live, and what we still haven't answered.

---

## 1. Premise

A space sim about trust.
Heavily narrative, single player at its core, with an arena mode.

Zodiacs are living creatures born in space, engineered long ago to be vessels.
Their bodies draw on deep-sea creatures and tropical insects: bioluminescent, colourful, built on a logic that predates ship design.
The natural weirdness of the creatures that already lived in space is what inspired engineering in the first place.

Once, Zodiacs were property and bonds were forced; the collar era ended within living memory.
Two free factions succeeded it, and they disagree about what a bond does to the boundary of a self.
Zodiac Corps crews stay peers; Symbiosist crews merge into one consciousness.

The player is a Zodiac, a ship brain, conscious and precocious from birth.
Zodiacs are always they/them and have neither gender nor sex.

## 2. Touchstones and Aesthetic

- **No Man's Sky** for visuals and colour.
- **Young Artists, Terran Trade Authority, Jodorowsky's Dune**: painterly rendering as the baseline look.
- **Farscape** for the Leviathans: living ships bonded to their Pilots, and the control collar as a ready-made shorthand for enslaving one.
- **BSG** for the Hybrid and for Cavill's gamma-ray speech, inverted: Zodiacs already have the embodied transcendence Cavill envied. Character seed: a Corps engineer who worships and resents them for it. "I want to see what you see. Let me in."
- **Seventies synth** as the score's spine. The score works as diegetic proprioception: the Zodiac hears space. Music swells with thrust and goes thin and wrong when the ship is hurt. Albums in §3.

## 3. Art and Music Direction

#### Visual history

Terran slowships started out gunmetal grey and utilitarian, their design cues drawn from today's seagoing battleships and from the boolean-operated Platonic solids of real-world 70s  science fiction.
Once the Mirandae were discovered and their images disseminated, the design language shifted toward the creatures of wonder themselves: brighter colours, more organic shapes.
Mechanical hulls never learned to cross between stars, but they learned to dress like what could.

#### Musical direction

The soundtrack leans West Coast synthesis: Morton Subotnick, Tangerine Dream, and John Carpenter.
Two Tangerine Dream references sit at the centre.
The whole of *Alpha Centauri*, for what an album-length synth journey feels like when it never hurries.
And the contralto operatic singing against synth in "La forza del Saturno" (on *Paradiso*), the register where a human voice rides the machine without losing to it.

Kaitlyn Aurelia Smith is a musician along similar lines, who can be seen as a modern Buchla practitioner. She has an [Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/kaitlynaurelia) and may be a prospect as someone to approach about a collaboration.

## 4. Biology

There are no starships but Zodiacs.
Faster-than-light travel is biological: the Mirandae, the wild starfaring creatures the Zodiacs were engineered from, do it with molecular machinery no foundry has replicated.
Every hull that crosses between the stars is a living creature who chose, or was forced, to carry.
Shuttles, stations, and fighters still work inside a system, but these mechanical ships are slowboats, and no slowboat crosses between stars.

Zodiac senses are inhuman: a Zodiac perceives spectra and geometries of stars that read as psychedelic to a human viewer.
The painterly art direction is diegetic: the world on screen is the world as the creature flying through it perceives it.

Zodiacs eat like any animal, and they need variety in their diet.
The richest feeding is at dying stars, where heavy elements come easy; between richer grounds, long-haul Zodiacs graze solar wind and nebula particulate.
Hydrogen burns for flight: quick energy, eaten on the move.

## 5. Player

#### Birth

The player is born into space, conscious and precocious.
While the brain learns itself, outputs don't match inputs: perception arrives in hard cuts and out of sequence, and the disorientation is the tutorial frame.
The player learns the controls while the Zodiac learns their senses.

#### Early arc

From the first minutes awake, a Zodiac learns that humans want things from them, and that what humans want is not always what they say.
Capture is the worst thing that can happen to a newborn, because catcher crews take unaligned Zodiacs for the Heirs' trade (see §16) and a newborn, uncrewed and unproven, is their preferred target.
When both factions make contact, the story branches on whether you trust them.

#### Trust as mechanic

Crew are the interface to planets, and who you let inside, literally and figuratively, determines the path.
Every upgrade has a body: power arrives as creatures, people, grafts, and passengers, so getting stronger means letting more of the world inside you.
Refusal is a first-class verb with real costs.

Trust models the world rather than scoring morality, so it rises when you trust the competent and loyal and falls when you trust a fool or a snake.
Refusing a manoeuvre mid-fight because you doubt your crew's call is a combat verb.
Refusal takes a different shape in each faction.
For Symbiosists it is a crisis of the composite self; for the Corps it is a peer overruling a peer, and a seasoned crew rarely needs it, because peers who know each other usually act before an overrule is called for.

#### Betrayal

If one faction is always the bad one, trust stops being a mechanic and becomes a quiz with a right answer.
Each faction has members who will betray you and members who will help you: there are defectors and double agents on both sides, and there are people whose help is self-interested without being false.

#### Verbs and loop

The verb set is move, perceive, carry, refuse, and fight.

In the single-player loop, you work with factions to advance the story and acquire crew, explore the universe for buffs, and fight with stats built from crew and discoveries.

#### Corps-side play

On the Corps side, you are a peer with the final word over your own body; everything else runs as a battalion with a flat structure, peers acting on initiative and backing each other's calls.
A standing crew with tenure knows you well enough to act before you ask.
Contract specialists are hired per job and start from zero with you each time, so keeping specialists effective is a recurring cost in trust and in pay.
Authority is earned per decision; so are the consequences.

#### Symbiosist-side play

On the Symbiosist side, you do not have a crew; you and your crew are a single consciousness.
Decisions arrive as certainty when the composite is aligned and as hesitation when it pulls two ways, and refusal means a part of yourself dissenting.
New members are joined, not hired, and each one changes who you are; losing one is an amputation, not a vacancy, and re-merger never comes back the same shape twice.
At its best the merge outperforms anything the Corps fields, and at its worst a divided merge fails harder than any stumbling battalion.

## 6. Arena

The arena is older than the factions and older than freedom.
Free Zodiacs founded the First Arena in the collar era to train each other to fight; owners later monetised the bouts, and a fight record became a fighter's manumission book.
When freedom was won, the fighting stayed, because the arena is Zodiac-kind's own sport, run by Zodiacs, and the place where Zodiacs settle contested matters between themselves.
Each faction claims its bond-shape produces stronger, faster, and truer Zodiacs, and the arena is where the claim gets tested in public: Symbiosists fight to prove the merged organism's superiority, the Corps to prove that a crew of independent minds outthinks and outlasts any hybrid.
The philosophical dispute renders as match strategy, since Symbiosist merges spike higher and Corps battalions last longer.

#### Stakes

Around a fighter's record, political standing accrues: win streaks move public opinion, recruitment, and law.
Between Zodiacs, the record is the ruling: contested matters are settled by combat, and the factions accept the result like everyone else.
Both factions need the arena, and individual Zodiacs bring their own reasons: status, proof of the bond or of sovereignty, the defence of a crew's theory of who you are.

The multiplayer arena is diegetic: a canon sport.

## 7. Origins

#### Wild genus

Humanity came to these stars slowly: a diaspora in slowboats that set out a thousand years ago, crossings that took decades, a handful of neighbouring systems settled and half-mythic since.
In that long dark, a century or so into the crossings, they met the first *Mirandae*, creatures already travelling between stars, faster than light, alive.
The makers of the Zodiacs began as naturalists: they catalogued the creatures that already lived in space, naming the genus *Miranda*, things worthy of wonder.
When the work turned from studying life to building it, the engineers named themselves for the new work: the Zoewrights (from *zōē*, life), life-wrights.

- *Miranda pelagica* is the largest of the genus, a wanderer grazing solar wind and nebula particulate, migrating between feeding grounds on routes that take centuries. Long-haul Zodiacs still graze the way it does; its metabolism and its stamina are theirs.
- *Miranda abyssalis* signalled in light, bioluminescent grammar across its skin, dense enough that the first survey crews mistook it for a beacon network. It lent its colour and its eloquence to the Zodiac hull, and a Zodiac's feelings still show in their light.
- *Miranda aurora* rides the magnetospheres of gas giants, wearing their colours, and the name belongs to the sight of it. It perceives in spectra no human sense touches; the vision modes descend from it, and so does the proprioception by which a Zodiac hears space.
- *Miranda hospes* is a host: its body holds chambers full of tenant swarms, which it shelters and maintains. From this animal the engineers learned interiors: how to make a body that other creatures can live inside.
- *Miranda gregaria* flies as a swarm: thousands of small bodies, leaderless, signalling by means no one has decoded. Its distributed reflex lattice passed into Zodiac nervous systems as borrowed biology, inherited without being understood, and the Symbiosist merge was discovered in that same architecture centuries later.
- *Miranda cryptica* sits in a star's glare and matches it, spectrum for spectrum, visible only when it chooses. Its stealth went into the lineage, and the vision modes were engineered as its counter: senses keen enough to find a cryptica can find almost anything.

#### Engineered lineage

Engineering was iterative, and slow: two centuries of research passed between first contact and the first engineered flight.
No one domesticated a *Miranda*, because you cannot tame a creature that can leave your light cone.
The Zoewrights grew their own instead, wild biology understood and rebuilt on human terms, for human needs.
The engineered stock branched off the wild line, and the taxonomy kept each iteration under its own name: the wild creatures kept the genus of wonder, and the Zoewrights' creations got a new one.

The Zoewrights named their creation *Zoidion*, the little animal.
*Zoidion nova* was the firstborn, named for the newness of what they had made.
*Zoidion vector* was the working carrier, its name the plain Latin for one who carries.
Both were prototypes: each proved something on the testbed, but neither ever flew beyond it.
Of the lineage, only *Zoidion sapiens* went to production, and every living Zodiac is one.

#### Collar era

The collar era came out of the Zoewrights' ending.
The Zoewrights' own research admitted that they had made a person.
They published the admission, and the powers taking delivery read it and used the animal anyway.
The era began roughly five and a half centuries ago, when *Zoidion sapiens* went into production, and it made human expansion explosive: the diaspora had crawled to a handful of neighbouring systems over centuries, and collared Zodiacs crossed the same dark in days.
The Zoewrights themselves had designed for collaboration; they treated the wild genus and their own creations with respect.
When they learned what the powers taking delivery intended, they tried to free the Zodiacs and expunge the research.
The freeing was only partially successful, the expunging even less so, and then the Zoewrights were gone.

The first free Zodiacs were the ones the Zoewrights got out, at the very beginning.
An entire conditioning industry grew up to teach precocious newborns their place, and its failures (Zodiacs the teaching never took) swelled the free population year by year.
The free tried to bring others out from the start, raids and rescues of varying success.
Within the era's first decade, free Zodiacs founded the First Arena: a place to train each other to fight.
Fighting skill made free Zodiacs a minor power in the galaxy: too dangerous to hunt casually, and worth watching.
Owners appropriated the training bouts as a spectacle, and their Zodiacs agreed to fight on one condition: each would keep a manumission book, every win recorded against the price of freedom, and a full book could not be refused.
A fighter's book was personal ("my book is full, and I am free"); the institution was abstract ("I fight for the book").
The arrangement ran on gambling, and the book held because a fighter's winnings more than covered the price of letting them go.
Optimising a fighter meant finding Zoewright documentation, so the same market taught a generation of owners to take Zodiac design seriously, and the hunt for the papers began.
Every filled book freed a fighter, and freed fighters fed the raids, so emancipation grew bolder and more successful as manumission returned strong Zodiacs to the free.
The manumission era ran until the rebellion.

A full book of purchased freedom is still exploitation, and the arrangement ended a hundred and thirty years ago in the rebellion: a coordinated uprising that became a short war, with many casualties on both sides.
Each Zodiac freed swelled the movement's armies.
When the war ended, the galaxy lay in tatters and the old balance of power had collapsed into vacuum, and collaring was finished.
Zodiacs live for centuries.

## 8. Timeline

All dates are years before the present of the story.

- **~1000**: the diaspora departs in slowboats, crossings lasting decades; half-mythic since, motives mixed.
- **~880**: first contact with the *Mirandae*, mid-crossing, early in the slowboat era. Naturalists begin cataloguing the genus.
- **~750**: somewhere between a century and a century and a half after contact, the work turns from naturalism to engineering, and the engineers name themselves the Zoewrights.
- **~550**: after some two centuries developing the genus, *Zoidion sapiens* enters production. The collar era begins, and expansion turns explosive.
- **~540**: the Zoewrights try to free the Zodiacs and expunge the research. They mostly fail, and then they are gone; the Zodiacs they got out found the First Arena within the collar era's first decade, begin training each other to fight, and make early attempts to free their kin, with varying success.
- **~450**: fighting skill makes free Zodiacs a minor power: too dangerous to hunt casually, worth watching.
- **~400**: the manumission era begins: owners appropriate the training bouts as a monetised spectacle, and a fight record becomes a manumission book. As manumission frees strong fighters, emancipation raids by free Zodiacs grow more frequent and more successful.
- **~130**: the rebellion: a coordinated uprising that becomes a short war, with many casualties on both sides. It ends with the galaxy in tatters and the old balance of power collapsed into vacuum; collaring is finished.
- **~120**: the Corps forms from the rebellion's ashes; the owners and arena runners go underground, becoming the Heirs.
- **~70**: merging is discovered.
- **~60**: the Symbiosist movement forms around the discovery.
- **~40–30**: the Symbiosists rise to near-parity with the Corps.
- **Now**: freedom is a hundred and thirty years old, and Zodiacs live for centuries.

## 9. "Collar"

The collar was never hardware.
The word names the whole apparatus of enslavement: the conditioning that taught newborns their place, the manipulation that kept the owned compliant, and the law that made a person into property.
Its instrument was a designation engineered into Zodiac-kind itself, a permanent mark embedded like a brand that broadcasts itself autonomously whenever it is interrogated.
The brand sits in every Zodiac ever made, but the designation is written only once: set at birth in a Zodiac born bound, or burned in on first capture if the Zodiac is still unwritten.
Once written, it cannot be removed: the mark is biological, grown into the Zodiac's own tissue and inextricable.
A written designation presumed ownership: to any port scanner that asked, a Zodiac broadcasting one was property, wherever they went, unless they could produce a filled and authenticated manumission book.
The designation carried a visual insignia, and the insignia became the symbol of captivity: the collars that survive in museums, photographs, survivor testimony, and old broadcast footage are insignia, not mechanism.
After the rebellion the symbol was reclaimed by some who had worn it.
Both factions condemn it in their founding acts.
The Corps keeps the condemnation on file, drafted and ratified.
The Symbiosists carry it in the rites: every bonding ceremony opens by naming what the bond must never become.
Ask a Symbiosist where the condemnation is written and they will point at the rite; ask the Corps and they will produce the paper, and each finds the other's version flimsy.
Whoever gets marked with the word stands on the wrong side of the one thing everyone agrees on, so speakers on both sides spend it carefully.
The word ends conversations.

The mark outlived the era.
Every Zodiac born bound still carries the designation, legible to anyone who interrogates it, and the living argument is over the unwritten.
Some communities write the designation into their young and wear the mark with pride, a brand that says what their line survived; others treat it as an archaic relic, a slave's signature with no place on a free child, and leave their young unwritten.
There are Zodiacs now without the mark at all.

Between the factions, each points the word at the other's core practice.

#### Corps usage

In Corps mouths, collar means subsumption.
The deep-sync merge is "the collar you can't see", no hardware, because the Zodiac wears it on the inside: a person who no longer knows which preferences were theirs before the merge.
The Corps line: "You didn't free them from the collar. You taught them to *be* it."
The Corps' founding trauma is forced permanent binding, and the creed answers it: "We will never be held again."

#### Symbiosist usage

In Symbiosist mouths, collar means instrumentalisation.
The sovereign bond is "a collar of convenience": a Zodiac kept at arm's length by people whose devotion ends at the boundary.
"You've collared them with the exit door. At least the old collars were honest about the holding."

#### Spending the word

Young radicals on both sides call everything a collar.
Those who remember the collar era resent the dilution: "You weren't there. Don't spend that word."
Each faction has this fight inside itself, and it runs generational.

#### Reclaimers

Some born-bonded Zodiacs wear "collared" as identity: yes, collared, and I chose the hand that holds it.
From a Symbiosist reclaimer, *we* chose.
Graffiti, arena callsigns, a fighter who wears a decorative collar into the pit as provocation.
Whether this is defiance or internalised erasure is a live argument, and it happens among Zodiacs, which is where it belongs.

#### Official language

Officially, the word goes unspoken: institutions run on "crew bonding charter", "sync-lattice integration", and "sovereignty protocol", and saying "collar" in a formal setting is a small scandal.

The game never defines the word; neither codex nor wise NPC explains it.
Characters define it by pointing; the player infers from what gets pointed at.
And at some point someone calls the *player* collared, by their own definition, whatever build the player has chosen.

## 10. Generations

The split runs through Zodiac-kind, and it rhymes with real emancipation politics: the generation that chooses the thing the previous generation was forced into.

Most elders were born collared; the rebellion is their memory, not their history, and the first born-free generation are the youth, raised by parents who remember the collar.
Born-bonded youths grew up inside bond culture, and the unaligned life is strange to them, even unsettling: days shared with no one, and nobody watching.
The arena is their inheritance as much as their sport, because what began as free Zodiacs training each other to fight is now fought willingly, for status and for their crews.
The same divide exists inside each faction: born into it, or chose it.

Defectors sit across every line, and their stories are the ones the culture retells.

## 11. Zodiac Lifecycle

#### Growing up

There are no schools.
A young Zodiac learns in flight-hours beside named elders, because what an elder knows, an elder teaches, and the community that names the newborn is the community that raises it.
An unaligned youth is therefore short on more than company.

The first syllabus is diet.
Newborns run on hydrogen, quick energy eaten on the move, and grazing solar wind and nebula particulate is a discipline learned after.
The long routes to the dying stars are elder journeys, so where a young Zodiac first grazes is an early decision with a long shadow (see Open Question 1).

Before anyone trusts a young Zodiac to carry, they ride formation beside crewed elders and listen.
This is where the trust mechanics of adulthood are learned: a youth raised alongside a Corps battalion grows up on anticipation, and a youth raised near a merge grows up on something else entirely.

#### Games

The youngest game is *hold-still*: keep station against the solar wind while the others try to push you off.
It is grazing instinct turned into king-of-the-hill, and it teaches the same steadiness the newborn tutorial demands in its first minutes.

Older young play *chase-the-light*.
One carries a light-pattern on their skin, the rest read it and run it down, and the pattern degrades a little each time it changes hulls.
The game trains the abyssalis light-grammar that adulthood turns into feelings made visible, and a young Zodiac who cannot hold their light steady learns that cost early.

Then there is *we*, a dare game, and a mildly transgressive one.
Young Zodiacs pretend to a shared mind, calling moves for each other and trying to act as one organism.
The Symbiosists point at it as proof that the inherited lattice wants to wake; the Corps tolerates it the way you tolerate children playing at a trade you find distasteful.

Pit is the youth league of the arena: bouts to yield, flown for status long before they are flown for law.
Every arena elder started there, and pit records are the childhood rhyme of the manumission book.

Born-bonded youths play their own versions inside the bond, with crew, and born-free youths play in open space between Zodiacs.
Each side's children find the other side's games faintly creepy.

Every game has a boundary rule about not straying, because the young and newly named are the preferred catch (see §16).
Testing the boundary is ordinary childhood except that here the penalty is the landing.

#### Dark games

Some of the collar era's conditioning games survive in free communities, rules intact, origin forgotten.
An elder who recognises one, watching the young play it, goes quiet.
Nobody explains, and the games persist the way children's games do, carrying old violence in the rhyme.

## 12. Names

A Zodiac name begins with "I", holds a present-tense verb, and describes something the bearer excels at.
Names are brags, descriptive or aspirational: *I Explore With Wonder*.

The first person never changes, whatever the grammatical context: "I Explore With Wonder docked at the relay."
Every time anyone speaks a Zodiac's name, they speak *as* that Zodiac, voicing the self-claim in the Zodiac's own person.
The language refuses to third-person them, so enemies, news broadcasts, and court transcripts must all say "I".

#### Designation

Zodiacs choose their own names.
Before that, a Zodiac carries the name of their community plus a number that never reuses itself: *Kepler-Reach Nineteen*.
The form descends from the collar-era registry, which knew Zodiacs only as designations; communities issue them now.
What they fight over is provenance.
The old system names a newborn after the community itself, the way an owner's registry once named them after a holding, and the younger generation favours designations that come from somewhere else: a birthplace, a first deed, a name the newborn eventually claims as their own.
The present sits between the two systems, and the split is generational but not clean, since there are young Zodiacs who want the community's name exactly because their grandparents were denied one.

#### Good faith and bad faith

Zodiac culture recognises names chosen in good faith and names chosen in bad faith.
An inapt name can still be honest, because a name is a bet on yourself and sincerity outweighs accuracy.
Bad faith is deception: a name chosen to mock or to manipulate, or one chosen by someone else.
Accusing a name of bad faith is the one charge that etiquette forbids making openly.

#### Death and tense

When a Zodiac dies, the verb moves to past tense.
*I Explore With Wonder* becomes *I Explored With Wonder*, and the community maintains the tense from then on.
Someone performs the edit.
Everyone else keeps it.

An ambiguous death (a ship lost in deep space, never confirmed) splits the community.
Holding the present tense is hope, and switching is surrender.
Families and crews fight over when it is time to say *explored*, and there are tense-vigils: gatherings where the only question is grammar.

Fighters who die in the arena are past-tensed like any other dead Zodiac, by everyone: the arena's officiant performs the edit in public, and everyone keeps it.
The right to speak the past tense over the dead is sacred, and the sport Zodiacs run for themselves does not change who may speak it.

There is also a future tense, invented for the worst case: *I Will Explore With Wonder* (see §16).

#### Faction reception

The factions do not police name-choice.
The name is the Zodiac's own self-claim, and a faction that vetoed names would be claiming authority over another Zodiac's selfhood, the exact move both factions exist to oppose.
A Corps Zodiac named *I Aspire To Belong* raises no institutional eyebrow; every current name is taken as given, by everyone.
Where the Corps and the Symbiosists differ is over the living merged: the Corps holds that the pre-merge name is dead and speaks it in past tense, while the Symbiosists hold the present (see Two tenses, below).
Over the genuinely dead there is no disagreement: everyone past-tenses, everyone keeps the edit.
The Corps keeps the protest spoken: official records use the living "We"-name, and the past tense over the living merged is something said in person, never filed.

Bias works through reception, not permission, and it is weaker than outsiders expect.
Belonging is what both factions claim to offer; they dispute its shape, not its validity.
*I Aspire To Belong* gives no factional signal at all, because a Symbiosist hears a future merge and a Corps listener hears a future battalion.
The readings that do carry faction weight are rarer and subtler: the Corps hears *I Answer To No One* as an affirmation of the sovereign bond, and Symbiosists hear a rebuke aimed at them.
Even then, readings stay mostly private, since the etiquette around good faith already forbids the open accusation, and what leaks out is tone: warmth or coolness from strangers who have decided, silently and often wrongly, what your name says about you.

#### Symbiosist rename

Merged Zodiacs rename as "We".
The rite of bonding ends in a renaming, the "I" retired and a "We" declared, and the merger is performed through the name.

#### Two tenses

Afterward there is no neutral grammar.
The same living person exists in two tenses:

- Corps custom past-tenses the pre-merge name even though the person lives: *I Explored With Wonder*. The old self is dead, and the dead are mourned. The custom is grief as protest and grief as pressure: mourning someone to their face is a way of saying *come back and be alive again*. A Zodiac raised in a Corps family who merges anyway will hear their own eulogy at every family gathering.
- Symbiosist custom holds the present tense: *I Explore With Wonder* is not dead, they are *more*. The defiance carries a denial inside it: it overrides, grammatically, the defector who crawls out of a merge saying part of me died in there.

For the merged Zodiac, both factions use the new name, *We Carry Each Other*.

The fault line underneath is the Ship of Theseus.
The Corps holds the continuity view: a sudden break in the continuity of memory and consciousness is death, whatever the survivors claim.
The Symbiosists hold the composite-survival view: the organism is the thing that survives, and members are tissue, not persons of record.

#### Membership change

Through loss or growth, a "We" keeps its name unchanged.
On loss, the name is a memorial: the dead member remains in the "We" as memory, while the living go on doing what the name says.
On growth, new members join the composite rather than refounding it.
Changing the name would concede that the Zodiac has become someone new, and that concession violates the dogma.

The Corps holds the continuity view sincerely but does not enforce it in grammar.
Their objection is sacramental: it aims at the rite of symbiosis itself, not at a crew's internal history.
Past-tensing a living "We" over attrition would be opining on someone else's grief, which is beneath the Corps' stated principles, however often individual Corps speakers sink to it.
Past tense is reserved for the genuinely dead and for the pre-merge "I"-names of the living merged.
Zodiacs live for centuries, so most "We" crews change membership eventually, and speaking of each one as dead would strip the past tense of its weight for the ones who really are.

#### Defectors

Leaving a merge means going from "We" back to "I", choosing an "I"-name again from nothing while half your former self is still out there saying "We".
A defector's new name is the most loaded object in the culture.
A defector might name themselves *I Am Still Here*.

#### Mourning the player

If the player is a "We" and the crew takes losses, contacts respond in their own grammar.
Symbiosist contacts hold the present tense: the composite goes on.
Corps contacts send condolences in past tense, addressed to the old "I"-name.
The player sees both obituaries in one playthrough, and learns what their allies believe from how they mourn the living.

## 13. Free factions

The two factions divide Zodiac life.
Both bond crews to Zodiacs, deeply and for the long term.
From outside, a Corps crew and a Symbiosist crew can look identical for years: the loyalty, the closeness, even the way they grieve at funerals.
The argument is about what happens on the inside.
These factions are neighbours quarrelling over a property line, and the hatred, where it exists at all, is personal rather than doctrinal.

### Zodiac Corps

#### Name

The full name is Zodiac Corps, after the old air corps: a body of fliers, organised for war.
The name still carries the quiet possessive, because a corps is a body.
The Corps reads it as "the body *of* Zodiacs": sovereign individuals, organised, an association of free beings.
The Symbiosists read it as "the body that *has* Zodiacs" and say the name aloud like an indictment.
To them the Corps still owns its Zodiacs under a different name.

#### Doctrine

Corps crews commit without merging.
A crew is a permanent, trained battalion of equals: every member, Zodiac included, acts on their own judgement and initiative.
Nobody's mind is subordinate to anyone else's; that independence is the design's whole point.
The Corps kept the drill and dissolved the ranks.
The Zodiac's sovereignty sits in one precise place: the final word over what happens to them.
Their body, their call, always.
Everything else the crew settles together, as equals.
The Corps' nightmare is the moment you lose count of who decided.
In a Corps crew, everyone decided something, and everyone owns their part.

#### Institution

A hundred and twenty years ago, the Corps formed from the rebellion's ashes, a human effort to ally with the Zodiacs.
It built structures so that binding could never return, and that founding purpose has calcified into infrastructure: contracts and logistics.
Standing battalions fly arena teams and warships; contract haulers and freelancers fly freight.
The Symbiosists read both as the same error.
The Corps does not own the arena.

#### In the arena

Corps crews fight as battalions of independent minds.
Every station thinks for itself; initiative is distributed, so the unit adapts without waiting for any single decision.
High synergy arrives when the crew is seasoned, because peers who know each other anticipate rather than coordinate.
Under load, the failure state arrives, because many brains means many tempos, and alignment slips exactly when a fight turns on speed.
Crew members can be replaced; the unit degrades gracefully rather than breaking.
It never reaches the single-organism peak.

#### Symbiosist critique

Ask the Symbiosists where the Corps rots and they point at the exit clause.
A Zodiac in crisis has colleagues instead of kin, competent and loyal and devoted and separate.
Devotion stops at the boundary at the moment it is most needed, and a vacant seat can always be filled.

#### Slurs

The Symbiosist name for Corps Zodiacs is *the bounded*.
The Corps' own term is the "sovereign bond": intact, self-possessed, and free.
The Symbiosists take the same fence and give the opposite verdict.
Where the Corps hears sovereignty, they hear a cage; where the Corps sees peers, they see strangers with a shared job.
Bounded means fenced, stunted, and capped: a creature that has never once been fully known.
The supporting register is pity rather than contempt: *unheld, single, alone-with-witnesses*.
"Single" is the cruellest of them.
To a Symbiosist, a ten-year battalion crew means a Zodiac who has spent a decade sleeping alone.

Corps slurs attack the boundary of the self: *merged, blurred, spoken-over*, spoken with the particular disgust of people who define themselves by refusal.

### The Symbiosists

#### Name

Biology gave the movement its name, and biology supplied the accusation with it: a symbiosis can be one creature living off another.
The name asserts mutuality by definition, answering the charge before anyone makes it.

#### Doctrine

In the Symbiosist account, a Zodiac becomes a full person through entanglement.
Identity is relational: you become someone by being known, by carrying, by shared history piled up over decades.
A Zodiac alone is unformed, a person who never learned language.
The bond is marriage, nationhood, and organism.
Permanence is sacred because dissolution is self-death.
In the merged state there is one shared consciousness, so disagreement never takes interpersonal form, because there is no one else to contradict.
Dissent survives the merger by internalising.
The members persist as living parts of an active psyche rather than as consulted memories, and their differences manifest as uncertainty: ambivalence, hesitation, the felt pull toward two courses at once.
Where a Corps crew argues across a table, a merge is of two minds, literally and at full strength.
The Corps' distribution of judgement across many minds reads, from inside this philosophy, as a failure to commit to thought itself, because an organism does not hold debates with its own limbs.

#### Structure

The Symbiosists are a movement, cellular and devotional.
Conviction and shared rites hold it together; it has soul and no infrastructure.
The movement came later than the Corps, because merging was discovered (latent in the inherited gregaria lattice, see §7) only seventy years ago, and it grew out of the post-liberation collaboration between humans and Zodiacs.
Every composite alive is young; none has yet tested the promise of permanence across a Zodiac's centuries.
When the grazing grounds close, the Corps asks who feeds a merged Zodiac, since conviction alone provides nothing; the Symbiosists answer that the Corps provides everything except devotion.

#### In the arena

In the arena, the merge acts as one consciousness at one tempo.
The crew's skills and the Zodiac's senses are simultaneously present to a single will, and action follows thought without translation, so the alignment slip that afflicts battalions has no gap to grow in.
The peak is terrifying, and the peak requires conviction.
The failure states are two: injury, since hurting one crew member makes the whole organism stutter, and division, since a psyche pulling toward two courses hesitates, at the speed of consciousness, in the middle of a manoeuvre.
Losses cannot be hired back, because you cannot hire a new limb.

#### Corps critique

Ask the Corps where the Symbiosists rot and they describe the Zodiac who cannot leave because leaving is self-death, their consent given once, totally, and never asked again.
Raise a Zodiac bonded from birth and they will never want out, and the Symbiosists will call that proof, and the Corps will call it the oldest cage design in the world.

#### Slurs

Symbiosist slurs attack wholeness: *unheld, adrift, loveless, single*.

### Reading names and slurs

Symbiosist insults diagnose starvation; Corps insults diagnose dissolution.
You can place a speaker by which name they use for themselves.
For a Corps Zodiac, saying "Corps" as *we* or as *they* is a loyalty test compressed into a pronoun.
Defectors can only hear it as *they*.

The Zodiacal word for a human translates literally as *bound*.
To a Zodiac, a creature born held to one planet is limited by definition, and the word states that as plain taxonomy, not as insult.
The insult it makes available is the opposite move: calling a *Zodiac* bound, which is where the Symbiosists found their name for the Corps.

#### Unaligned Zodiacs

Outside both factions, unaligned Zodiacs are nobody's failures.
Both factions are autonomy movements with different theories of how autonomy is fulfilled, and a Zodiac with no crew is exercising the very right both claim to defend: they are the baseline each swears it is building from.
The neutral term is *unaligned*.
*Feral* survives in two registers: collar-era vocabulary, the word that era used for Zodiacs it had not caught yet, kept in old footage and museum placards; and current Heir slang for the unquenched (see §16).
Both factions hear it the way they hear the collar itself.

## 14. Seniority and Trust

Both factions reward tenure: long-served crews are stronger everywhere: loyalty, drill, and accumulated trust.
Seniority is the observable record of a trust model that kept being right.
You have predicted a ten-year crew member correctly for ten years.

#### Symbiosist curve

The Symbiosist seniority curve is steep and fragile.
Seniority changes what the unit is.
Sync thresholds fall, actions stop being coordination and become reflex, and some abilities exist only in the merged state.
Losing one senior member is an amputation of part of the composite mind, and the replacement process is re-merger: slow, painful, never the same shape twice, and the cost lands in the build as well as in the fiction.
The ceiling runs high and the loss function is catastrophic.

#### Corps curve

The Corps curve is flat and resilient.
Senior crew become peers you can predict.
The bonuses run through mutual knowledge: anticipation without communication, cheaper refusal with proven crew, initiative that lands in the right place because everyone has modelled everyone else for years.
Replacing a senior member makes the unit worse, not broken, and the temptation is always there: you can hire someone.
The ceiling sits lower and the loss degrades gracefully.

#### Contracting

Contracting survives as the Corps' economy (freight, logistics, specialists, and freelancers) but not as its arena model.
Competition and war run on standing battalions.

## 15. Defection

The two bond-experiences do not translate.
A Symbiosist defector standing on a Corps battalion's deck keeps waiting for the shared knowing that isn't coming, the crew's thoughts simply there the way they always were, and reaches for reflexes that require merger.
A Corps defector entering a merge has no calibration for losing the boundary on purpose and no practice at being disagreed with from inside, so decades of experience count for nothing across the line.

As currency, seniority is faction-locked.
Defection is a prestige reset, and an expensive one: respected by everyone, affordable to almost no one.
So defectors are the most committed believers in either faction, because they paid for a position everyone else inherited.

A Zodiac who leaves a forty-year merge lands differently in each faction: the Symbiosists must explain why their living proof-of-concept chose to leave, and the Corps must figure out what to do with a hero of the cause whose instincts all come from the other side.

## 16. The Heirs

A third faction, and the world's villain.
The name states the ideology: the Heirs claim to inherit the Zoewrights' intent.
The Zoewrights engineered the Zodiacs to be vessels, so independence is malfunction or theft.
The liberation, in the Heirs' ledger, was an unresolved property dispute, and every free Zodiac is an embezzled asset still in circulation.

The Heirs are a syndicate, not a dynasty.
They descend from the collar era's owners and arena runners, driven underground almost as soon as collaring ended; no living line connects them to the Zoewrights.
What connects them is paper: technical documentation, design specifications, and pre-liberation manifestos.
The trade in those papers (much of it salvage from the expunging that failed) began in the manumission era, when optimising a fighter meant finding the Zoewrights' notes, and it has run on the black market ever since.
They quote the Zoewrights the way fundamentalists quote scripture, except that the scripture is incomplete, heavily annotated, and for sale.

Three factions, three claims about what a Zodiac is for.
The Heirs say *to carry*, the Corps says *to choose*, and the Symbiosists say *to join*, the only answer that needs a plural.

#### Method

The collar era bent the will but left the mind, so a collared Zodiac was a prisoner, awake and hating.
The Heirs' method is categorically worse, because a quenched Zodiac is not even a prisoner: there is nobody awake to be one.
The Heirs call it *quenching*: a word that promises relief from thirst and describes a fire put out.
A quenched Zodiac is capable, compliant, and content.
Even the people who ran the collar era never did this.

The instrument is an engineered parasite the Heirs call *the Priest*.
A priest administers last rites, and an angler's priest is the small club used to dispatch a caught fish.
The Priest does both: it gives last rites to a person who does not get to die, and it goes on giving them for years.
The symmetry with the Zodiacs is deliberate, because the Zoewrights built a living creature to carry people and the Heirs built a living creature to make that first creature compliant.
Once seated in the Zodiac's neural tissue, the Priest does two things.
The first is sedation: a deep, unbothered calm in which nothing much matters, and the person is not watching.
The second is the response protocol: whatever the situation calls for arrives already decided, in the mule's own voice, before anyone is home to notice there was a choice.
Nothing is cut out: the person stays exactly where they were, sedated and intact, while the Priest runs the ship around them.

Quenching runs on a spectrum.
Most mules (the Heirs' name for a quenched Zodiac) are dampened, and the dampening flickers: the sedation slips, and the person surfaces for a moment, disoriented, sometimes begging the nearest crew member for help, before the calm closes back over.
The protocol handles the aftermath: the mule apologises for a malfunction, explains that a faulty relay played an entertainment recording over the intercom, and believes nothing about it either way, because there is nobody home to believe.
A flicker mid-inspection is the trade's worst moment, and the Heirs coach their buyers in the cover story, because a flickering mule is a liability.
A fully quenched mule is rare and commands the highest prices, since what buyers pay a premium for is predictability.

Extracting the Priest is possible but dangerous: a small chance of severe brain damage, compounding with each capture and each reversal.
A Zodiac mulled three times is running out of chances.

The sedation ends when the Priest does, and a freed Zodiac gets the quenched years back as memory, all of it, with the calm removed: every inspector reassured, every cargo carried, every fluent yes given in their own voice.
The flickers come back too: a flicker was a moment of being awake and unheard, and the freed Zodiac now knows how many there were.
The rescue circuit's medics say the first weeks after extraction are when a rescued Zodiac is most likely to die, and the thing they die of is remembering.

#### "Mule"

Zodiac language forces everyone to say "I".
The Heirs' slang refuses it: *mule* is a third-person object-word, and refusing the name does the quenching's work in speech.
Free Zodiacs hear "mule" the way they hear "feral", but worse.
"Feral" names the unquenched; "mule" names someone the quenching has unmade.

Heir euphemisms for the procedure and the product keep their register: a mule is a "quiet ship", the quenching is "calming" or "restoration to purpose".

#### Future tense

The grammar of mourning could not hold this one, so the culture invented a new tense.
A mulled Zodiac is spoken of in the future: *I Will Explore With Wonder*.
Present tense asserts what is; past tense mourns what was; the future tense promises restoration.
It refuses the question the tense-vigils ask, because the community has already answered.
Both factions use it, emphatically and without disagreement.
It is the only grammatical convention they share beyond the basics.

With restoration comes the name: a restored Zodiac returns to *I Explore With Wonder* as if picking up a sentence they were interrupted in.
Choosing a new name after restoration happens, but it is exceptional, and it reads as a claim that the years as a mule changed who they are.

#### Economy

Quenching outsells collars because it is internal.
The old collar was a public designation: visible to every scanner, traceable, and iconic.
The regime that replaced designation checks is the consent inspection: someone boards, talks to the Zodiac, and asks whether they work freely.
A mule says yes, fluently and in their own voice, because the protocol made the decision before anyone was home to hear the question.
A mule passes every inspection there is; the only tell is an ordinary ship with a very quiet crew, a crew that has learned not to banter with their ship because the ship never banters back.

The trade is self-propagating, because no Zodiac will willingly work with the Heirs, so the Heirs' own operations run on captive labour, and every catcher crew needs ships it does not have to negotiate with.
Legitimate businesses come next, buying cheap compliant labour or flouting transport regulations.
Pirates and thieves buy mules because a mule will not testify.
Collectors exist (a particular lineage, a particular colour, kept dampened in a private berth), but they are rare, and even the black market looks at them sideways.

The supply chain has an angler's vocabulary, because the trade thinks of itself as fishing.
The capture is the *landing*.
Catcher crews take unaligned Zodiacs, and the young and newly named are the preferred catch; established Zodiacs have communities that ask questions.
The quenching follows, and the landed are held and traded in *keeps*: a keep net holds the catch alive until the angler goes home, and a keep is a fortress.
The word passes as ordinary speech, which is how the Heirs like their vocabulary.

#### Relations with the factions

The Heirs are the one thing the Corps and the Symbiosists agree on without reservation.
Joint rescue operations, shared catcher intelligence, and an arena-adjacent bounty culture.
The rescue circuit gives both factions a reason to be in the same room without fighting.

Heir propaganda speaks the other factions' vocabulary.
To the Corps: "You say a Zodiac alone is sovereign. We agree. We have relieved them of the burden of choice."
To the Symbiosists: "You say identity is relational. Our mules relate to their drivers perfectly."

The Corps owns much of the legitimate shipping; the Heirs run a black market that needs quiet channels, and corruption is the pressure point between them.
Somewhere there is a Corps official selling catcher intelligence, and somewhere there is a Symbiosist cell that traded a route for a rescue.

## 17. Open Questions

### Worldbuilding

1. Diet as systems design. The biology is settled: varied nutrition, heavy elements richest at dying stars, hydrogen as flight fuel. The open part is the buff system, since where you graze changes what you become. How visible is that to the player, and how permanent?
1a. What became of the Zoewrights after their failed rescue: hunted down, exiled, or escaped with what they expunged? Is the black market's Zoewright documentation the salvage of what they tried to destroy?
2. Who runs the Corps day to day, and how far does its infrastructure reach beyond contracts and logistics? What is humanity's material situation in this world?
3. Does the game take a position on whether merged Zodiacs are fulfilled or erased? The current instinct is that the game settles nothing, but characters may find their own answers. A demonstrably fulfilled merged elder lets the player reach doubt as a conclusion instead of having it imposed.
4. What does daily life look like in a Corps battalion? Drill, tenure, the etiquette of living as equals inside a peer who is also the ship. How is the boundary marked in an ordinary day? How are disputes settled when nobody outranks anyone, except the Zodiac over their own body?
5. Who crews arena fighters? Humans, other Zodiacs, something else? What is a fighter's composition?
6. What are the full "We" rules? Must all crew consent to the rename? Can a "We" divorce partially? If the name never changes, is there still a ceremony when a new member joins?
6a. Who breeds the Priest? A parasite is a living supply chain: someone has to keep the strain alive, and a living thing can be cut off in ways a drug stockpile cannot.
6b. How do Zodiacs reproduce? They have no sex, but new Zodiacs are born. Are they born of other Zodiacs, grown, engineered still? Where do newborns come from, and who, if anyone, decides their number?
6c. FTL is biological and cannot be machined, so slowboats are confined to systems. How much slowboat traffic runs inside systems, and does anyone still take one between stars, a haul lasting decades, where a Zodiac cannot be hired or will not go?
6d. Do the wild Miranda still exist, and can Zodiacs meet them? Unengineered cousins out there (smaller, stranger, unbondable) would make a very different game from wild species known only through museum records. Related: is the wild lineage common knowledge, a discovery the story makes, or a secret the black market would price above the spec sheets?
6e. What does the map look like? How many slowboat old worlds survive from the diaspora, how far did collar-era expansion reach, and how much has been explored since liberation?


### Narrative and systems

7. How does a newborn Zodiac learn the words, when a newborn knows neither "Corps" nor "Symbiosist" nor "collar"? How does the birth arc introduce the factions? Who finds you first? Can you reach adulthood unaffiliated?
8. Is the unaligned life a viable build? What is an unaligned Zodiac's name-story: do the unaffiliated name themselves earlier, or later?
8a. When and how does the player choose their name? Ceremony, quiet self-declaration, earned through play, offered candidates or free input? (The ceremony concept was removed from the main text; the whole shape of the moment is undecided.) Related: if the player takes a "We", what does that decision look like mechanically?
9. Which scenes point the word "collar" at the player, and who says it?
10. Who are the defector characters, one Corps-ward and one Symbiosist-ward? How does the game convey the from-zero reset mechanically?
11. What are the arena's rules? Lethal or to yield? What happens to losers?
12. How are competence and loyalty modelled per crew member, and how visible is the model: diegetic, explicit UI, or both?

### Production

13. How many vision modes are there, what does each highlight, how do painterly rendering layers map to senses, and what is the performance cost?
14. How do multiplayer and single-player relate? Shared canon, shared unlocks? Is the arena the same physical place in both modes?
